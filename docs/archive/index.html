Issue · September 21, 2026

Can technical mitigation move fast enough, or do distribution and responsibility rules matter more?

India’s power sector kept emissions flat for two years even as demand rose, showing that solar and other clean power can now absorb incremental load, but new coal capacity still raises system costs through lower utilization hours; the policy implication is that power governance must go beyond capacity expansion to curb marginal coal generation while tackling rising emissions from steel, cement, and other hard-to-abate sectors.

Issue · September 14, 2026

Climate rhetoric is receding while governance costs rise: who will bear responsibility for transition and strain?

Carbon Brief dismantles 45 misleading claims by Reform UK on climate science, net-zero, and energy prices, showing how the party blames deindustrialization and living-cost pressures on climate policy while sidestepping the IPCC consensus on human-caused warming and the institutional costs of scrapping targets, expanding fossil fuels, and rewriting renewables contracts.

Issue · September 7, 2026

Both technical fixes and governance repairs are accelerating, but who pays for the externalities remains unresolved.

UK solar generation set a summer record under extreme heat and long daylight, showing that renewable deployment is beginning to turn climate stress into supply gains; the policy implication is that grid dispatch, storage, and interconnection rules must advance in parallel so weather-driven gains become system resilience rather than a short-lived output spike.

Issue · August 31, 2026

Shocks are being translated into both resilience and sovereignty; who gets to define “adaptation” is now the central contest.

The European Commission’s forthcoming Climate Resilience and Risk Management Integrated Framework should treat heatwaves not merely as emergency management, but as a single governance problem linking power-system resilience, adaptation finance, and regulatory coordination; otherwise member states will deepen vulnerability through fragmented responses and miss a window for institutionalization.

Issue · August 24, 2026

Who ultimately prices and constrains the transition: markets, states, or multilateral coordination?

E3G argues that amid geopolitical fragmentation, high interest rates, and shrinking development assistance, climate finance must shift from simple capital mobilization to a package centered on multilateral development banks, debt instruments, and risk-sharing mechanisms, or developing countries will be priced out of the transition.
